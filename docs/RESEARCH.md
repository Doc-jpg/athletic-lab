# Eli's Athletic Lab: research file

Compiled 2026-11-02. Purpose: the published guidance that the app's readiness engine, progression rules and defaults rest on. The athlete is a boy born in 2014 (age 12), travel baseball (shortstop, center field, rotation pitcher) and basketball. The app is a general wellness tool, not medical software, and nothing here is medical advice.

Each source section is split into "What the source says" (facts read from the page, with the URL) and "What the app encodes" (the design choice the app makes on that basis). Where a page could not be read, that is stated and nothing is invented.

## 1. American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP): resistance training for children and adolescents

**Primary document:** Stricker PR, Faigenbaum AD, McCambridge TM, AAP Council on Sports Medicine and Fitness. "Resistance Training for Children and Adolescents." Pediatrics, 2020;145(6):e20201011. doi 10.1542/peds.2020-1011. It replaces the 2008 AAP policy statement.

**Fetch status:** The full text on publications.aap.org and the doi.org link both returned client errors and could not be read. The content below comes from the AAP's own public summaries and handouts, plus two news summaries of the 2020 report. Numeric dosing (sets, reps, load increments) is NOT taken from the 2020 report, because that text was not read; it comes from the AAP patient-education handout and the older AAP policy statement, which are labeled separately.

### 1a. AAP news summary of the 2020 report (HealthyChildren.org)
URL: https://www.healthychildren.org/English/news/Pages/Guidance-on-Resistance-Training-for-Children.aspx

What the source says:
- "Children of all ages with proper supervision can benefit from resistance training." Strength can be built from about age 5 with hops and jumps; "resistance training consists of much more than weight-lifting."
- Age alone does not determine readiness. A child with more experience and skill has an older "training age" than a beginner of the same age.
- The report stresses proper technique and supervision.
- Combine resistance training with aerobic work and other skill-related fitness in a developmentally appropriate way.
- Each session should include dynamic warm-up exercises and finish with less intense stretching.
- Encourage adequate fluid intake and proper nutrition.
- Evaluate any illness symptom, injury sign or overuse sign before resuming training.
- Consult a medical professional first if there is uncontrolled high blood pressure, uncontrolled seizures, certain heart conditions or a history of chemotherapy.
- This page does not give reps, sets, frequency, progression, maximal-lift or growth-plate statements.

### 1b. AAP "Resistance training in youth: myth vs. fact" (aap.org)
URL: https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/physical-activity/resistance-training-in-youth-myth-vs.-fact

What the source says:
- Prepubertal children can get stronger; gains come mainly from better neuromuscular recruitment, not muscle size.
- "Well-designed resistance training programs have not been shown to have any negative effects on growth."
- "Resistance training is a safe activity with proper technique and supervision."
- With full range of motion it can improve flexibility and joint mobility.
- Bodyweight and light-band programs can be "an excellent foundation for resistance training."
- "Resistance training just twice per week has health benefits."
- Cites the 2020 Pediatrics clinical report and the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition.

### 1c. News summaries of the 2020 report
Healio (2020-05-26): https://www.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20200526/aap-kids-can-benefit-from-resistance-training-with-proper-supervision
Contemporary Pediatrics: https://contemporarypediatrics.com/view/aap-addresses-resistance-training

What the sources say (both attribute these to the 2020 report):
- "Use proper technique and supervision." "Assess training and provide real-time feedback to minimize risk and maximize benefits."
- "Include dynamic warm-up exercises and cool down with less intensive stretching."
- Address all major upper- and lower-body muscle groups; sport-specific exercises can be added to the core program.
- Count resistance training toward total training time and monitor that time to reduce overuse injury risk.
- Weightlifting exercises can be included under a qualified professional's direction.
- Educate athletes about the risks of performance-enhancing substances.
- Many children begin organized sports between 5 and 7, and the authors consider it reasonable to begin some form of resistance training then.
- Neither summary reports sets, reps, frequency, maximal-lift or growth-plate language from the 2020 report.

### 1d. AAP "Strength Training" handout (Care of the Young Athlete, HealthyChildren.org, 2011)
URL: https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/sports/Pages/Strength-Training.aspx
PDF: https://www.aap.org/globalassets/publications/coya/strength_training_final_secured.1.0.pdf

What the source says (AAP, copyright 2011, last updated 7/8/2011; older than the 2020 report but still the AAP's published numbers):
- Readiness: most children have the balance and postural control needed by about 7 or 8.
- "Practice all lifts without weights to make sure form and technique are correct." Add weight slowly once technique is mastered.
- "Gradually increase weights by no more than 10% per week."
- 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 15 repetitions; 2 to 3 times a week; 20 to 30 minutes; all major muscle groups including the core, full range of motion.
- "Warm up and cool down for at least 10 minutes." Avoid breath-holding or bearing down while lifting.
- "No 1-repetition maximums, maximum weights, or ballistic maneuvers should be performed before reaching skeletal maturity." Olympic lifting, powerlifting and bodybuilding "is not recommended for children."
- Supervised training with low weight and high repetitions is not harmful to growth plates; unsupervised programs risk "injury to the discs and growth plates of the spine."
- "Stop lifting at once if pain is felt."
- Coach-to-student ratio 1:10 or less; certified instructor.

### 1e. Older AAP policy statement (for the "8 to 15 reps" lineage)
"Strength Training by Children and Adolescents," AAP Committee on Sports Medicine and Fitness, Pediatrics 2001;107(6):1470-1472 (copy at https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Strength%20Training%20by%20Children%20and%20Adolescents.pdf). The 2008 revision on publications.aap.org could not be fetched.

What the source says:
- "When 8 to 15 repetitions can be performed, it is reasonable to add weight in small increments." Start with low resistance until technique is learned.
- Sessions of 20 to 30 minutes, "a minimum of 2 to 3 times per week"; no added benefit above 4 times a week.
- Preadolescents and adolescents "should avoid competitive weight lifting, power lifting, body building, and maximal lifts" until physical and skeletal maturity.
- Growth-plate (epiphyseal, apophyseal) injuries are uncommon and "believed to be largely preventable by avoiding improper lifting techniques, maximal lifts, and improperly supervised lifts."
- Programs "should include a warm-up and cool-down component."

### 1f. Nemours KidsHealth "Strength Training" (AAP-adjacent, reviewed March 2022)
URL: https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/strength-training.html
- Learn form with no weight first; add resistance only after form is solid and 8 to 12 reps are comfortable.
- One or two sets of 8 to 12 reps with light resistance for beginners; if 8 reps cannot be done with good form the weight is too heavy.
- 2 or 3 days a week with at least a day off between sessions; 20 to 30 minutes.
- Warm up 5 to 10 minutes; cool down with lighter activity and static stretching.
- "Most injuries happen because a child was goofing around and not supervised."

**What the app encodes from section 1:**
- Bodyweight and technique first. Every exercise is introduced with no load; load is a later unlock, never the starting point.
- Load progression is gated on demonstrated technique (parent or coach confirms) and moves in small steps. The app uses the AAP handout's ceiling of no more than 10 percent per week and treats the move from bodyweight to a 5 lb dumbbell as that first small step.
- Rep and set targets stay inside 1 to 3 sets of 6 to 15 reps (the overlap of AAP 8 to 15, KidsHealth 8 to 12, NSCA 6 to 15). Nothing is programmed to failure.
- No 1-rep max, no maximal loads, no ballistic barbell lifts, no powerlifting or bodybuilding-style work. Not available in any setting.
- Every session opens with a dynamic warm-up and closes with easy movement and stretching.
- Stop rule: pain, dizziness, illness, or feeling off ends the session and the app tells him to tell a parent. Returning after an injury or illness sign requires a parent's go-ahead.
- Strength days are 2 to 3 per week, never on back-to-back days (see section 3 for the 48 to 72 hour rule).
- Training time counts toward total weekly load (section 6).

## 2. CDC physical activity guidelines, ages 6 to 17

URLs: https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/children.html and https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/adding-children-adolescents/what-counts.html

What the source says:
- Children and adolescents 6 to 17 need "60 minutes or more of moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity each day."
- Most of it aerobic; vigorous-intensity aerobic activity at least 3 days a week.
- Muscle-strengthening at least 3 days a week (examples: climbing, push-ups, bodyweight or band exercises, playground climbing).
- Bone-strengthening at least 3 days a week (hopping, skipping, jumping, jump rope, running, sports with jumping or quick direction changes).
- Many activities count in more than one category; basketball and running cover aerobic and bone-strengthening at once.
- Children "usually don't need formal weightlifting programs until they become adolescents."

**What the app encodes:**
- Baseball practices and games, basketball, and free play are logged as activity and count toward the 60-minute day; the app does not add sessions on top of a full sport day to "hit a number."
- The app's short strength and coordination sessions supply the muscle-strengthening days; basketball and sprint work supply bone-strengthening days. On weeks with little sport, the app fills those in; on heavy sport weeks it does not duplicate them.

## 3. NSCA position statements and long-term athletic development

### 3a. 2014 International Consensus (Lloyd, Faigenbaum, Stone et al.), British Journal of Sports Medicine 2014;48:498-505
Copy read: https://g-se.com/uploads/blog_adjuntos/br_j_sports_med_2014_lloyd_498_505.pdf

What the source says:
- Supported "on the proviso that qualified professionals design and supervise training programmes."
- Chronological age is "a weak indicator of maturational status." Training age matters: "a technically proficient 10-year-old child should not be restricted to introductory training methods."
- "It is vital that the fundamentals of technical competency are prioritised at all times." "Once basic exercise technique is competent, then prescription should be progressed."
- Beginners: 1 to 2 sets at low to moderate intensity (60 percent of 1RM or less); for multi-joint lifts, fewer reps (1 to 3) with real-time feedback while learning. Progression: 2 to 4 sets of 6 to 12 reps at 80 percent of 1RM or less. Advanced phases exist but are conditional on technique holding.
- Rest about 1 minute between sets for most children; 2 to 3 minutes for power or skill work.
- "2 to 3 sessions per week on non-consecutive days is most appropriate." Allow "adequate time for rest and recovery." In season, one to three sessions depending on sport demands.
- Before puberty strength gains are mostly neural; during the adolescent spurt boys add lean mass. "Childhood is deemed to be a crucial time in which to develop motor skill competency." Resistance training improves jumping, running and throwing.
- Growth-plate fears "are not supported by scientific reports or clinical observations." About 77 percent of injuries are accidental and "most injuries are potentially avoidable with appropriate supervision." Targeted training can cut overuse injuries by roughly half.
- Avoid: excessive load at the expense of technique; "excessive training with inadequate recovery"; high-intensity metabolic programs with too little rest between sets; unqualified supervision; treating resistance training "simply as an addition to the overall sporting schedule."
- The document does not give a percent-per-week load rule and does not use the term peak height velocity.

### 3b. NSCA 2009 Youth Resistance Training position statement (Faigenbaum et al.)
URL: https://dxpprod.nsca.com/globalassets/about/position-statements/position_stand_youth_resistance_training---2009.pdf

What the source says:
- "Begin resistance training 2 to 3 times per week on nonconsecutive days," which "allows 48 to 72 hours between sessions" for recovery.
- Begin with "relatively light loads" and "1 to 3 sets of 6 to 15 repetitions" on strength exercises; power work 1 to 3 sets of 3 to 6 fast, controlled reps, not to failure.
- "Increase the resistance gradually (5 to 10 percent) as strength improves." Novices learn technique with light loads before adding weight.
- "5- to 10-minute dynamic warm-up period"; static stretching in the cool-down.
- Supervised by a qualified adult; if qualified supervision and safe equipment are unavailable, youth "should not perform resistance exercise."
- Readiness: "if a child is ready for participation in sport activities (generally age 7 or 8 years), then he or she is ready for some type of resistance training."
- Maximal testing only after a habituation period and under qualified supervision; unsupervised or improper max testing "should not be performed by children or adolescents under any circumstances."
- Growth cartilage can be hurt by repetitive microtrauma; past injuries were tied to poor technique, maximal lifts or no supervision; no prospective youth study has reported a growth-cartilage injury.
- Emphasize "fundamental movement patterns"; progress from simple to multi-joint movements as competence improves. Children "must not be treated as miniature adults." Periodize across the year with active rest between seasons.

Note on peak height velocity: the two position statements read here describe the adolescent spurt and maturation but do not lay out a PHV-based coordination rule. The widely taught LTAD idea that coordination can dip temporarily during the growth spurt is not quoted from these pages and is treated below as a design assumption, not a cited fact.

**What the app encodes from section 3:**
- Minimum 48 hours between comparable strength sessions (same body region, same movement pattern); the engine will not schedule two lower-body strength days back to back.
- Youth range 1 to 3 sets of 6 to 15 reps; power and jump work 1 to 3 sets of 3 to 6 quality reps, never to failure. Rest about a minute between sets.
- Progression order: technique, then reps within range, then sets, then a small load step (5 to 10 percent, or bodyweight to 5 lb). One variable changes at a time.
- In-season weeks run 1 to 2 short sessions; off-season weeks run up to 3.
- Training age, not birthday, drives unlocks. The app tracks sessions completed with good technique and unlocks progressions on that count.
- Fundamental movement skills (squat, hinge, lunge, push, pull, brace, hop and land, throw and catch) form the base library.
- Growth-spurt handling (design assumption, flagged as such): if the parent logs a height jump, the app leans toward coordination and landing-control work for a few weeks and holds load progressions.

## 4. Sports nutrition, supplements, creatine and hydration

### 4a. AAP on creatine and supplements for teens (HealthyChildren.org, Ask the Pediatrician, Sigrid Wolf MD, 2026-10-15)
URL: https://www.healthychildren.org/English/tips-tools/ask-the-pediatrician/Pages/my-15-year-old-wants-to-take-creatine-for-sports-performance-Is-creatine-safe-for-teen-athletes.aspx

What the source says:
- Teens should "think twice about creatine supplements." "Supplements are not shortcuts to maximize athletic performance." Young athletes do not need supplements; unneeded ones "simply waste money and time," and supplements are not FDA tested or regulated.
- Creatine can cause weight gain, dizziness, nausea, vomiting and diarrhea; claims about brain health or sleep loss lack solid support.
- "A food-first regimen works best," with plenty of protein from food; sleep "8+ hours of rest daily"; stay hydrated; muscle comes "naturally with appropriate training, recovery, nutrition, and sleep."
- This page does not state a hard "under 18" rule and does not mention protein powder specifically.

Supporting reporting: NPR (2017-01-02), https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2017/01/02/507478762/is-the-warning-that-creatines-not-for-teens-getting-through, says the AAP "specifically recommends against its use by adolescents" and that most creatine labels say not for anyone under 18. The AAP 2016 clinical report "Use of Performance-Enhancing Substances" (LaBotz, Griesemer, Pediatrics 2016) could not be fetched directly; a Healio summary (https://www.healio.com/pediatrics/practice-management/news/online/%7Bd6fe7836-9c21-419f-ae5f-83118aee8ed9%7D/aap-education-key-to-curbing-teen-use-of-performance-enhancing-substances) quotes it: "adherence to appropriate nutrition and training regimens produces comparable athletic gains," and early use of over-the-counter supplements "may be associated with an increased risk for use of anabolic steroids." The Nutrients 2021 review on creatine in children (PMC7922146) was blocked by a captcha and was not read.

### 4b. Hydration: AAP clinical report on sports drinks and energy drinks (Schneider, Benjamin; Pediatrics 2011;127:1182-1189)
Read via MDedge summary: https://mdedge.com/content/aap-kids-should-avoid-energy-and-sports-drinks and Nationwide Children's: https://www.nationwidechildrens.org/new-guidelines-for-sports-and-energy-drinks; Nemours KidsHealth (reviewed 2019): https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/power-drinks.html

What the sources say:
- "Water should be encouraged as the principal source of hydration for children and adolescents."
- Sports drinks are useful only for "prolonged, rigorous activity"; the AAP "recommends that sport drinks not be consumed after participating in short training or competition sessions." Rule of thumb from the secondary pages: more than an hour of vigorous work, or very hot and humid conditions.
- Energy drinks "have no place in a young person's diet"; caffeine levels can reach toxic amounts.
- "For most kids, drinking water before, during, and after playing sports will keep them hydrated."

**What the app encodes from section 4:**
- No creatine and no routine protein powder in the default program. The app does not sell, suggest or track supplements. If a parent asks, the app points to the AAP page and to food first.
- Daily nutrition nudges are food-based: protein at meals, carbohydrate around games, fruit and vegetables, no numbers that would read as a diet prescription.
- Water is the default hydration prompt. A sports-drink prompt appears only when the logged sport block is over 60 minutes of hard work or the day is logged as hot, and it is phrased as optional. Energy drinks are never suggested and the app says why once if asked.
- Sleep is treated as the first recovery tool (section 5).

## 6. Workload management: pitch counts, cumulative load, overuse and specialization

### 6a. MLB Pitch Smart
URLs: https://www.mlb.com/pitch-smart/pitching-guidelines, https://www.mlb.com/pitch-smart/pitching-guidelines/ages-9-12, https://www.mlb.com/pitch-smart/pitching-guidelines/ages-13-14

What the source says (pitches in a game and required rest days):

| Age | Daily max | 0 days rest | 1 day | 2 days | 3 days | 4 days |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 11 to 12 | 85 | 1 to 20 | 21 to 35 | 36 to 50 | 51 to 65 | 66 to 85 |
| 13 to 14 | 95 | 1 to 20 | 21 to 35 | 36 to 50 | 51 to 65 | 66 to 95 |

Ages 9 to 12 recommendations, quoted: "Do not exceed 80 combined innings pitched in any 12-month period." "Take at least 4 months off from throwing every year, with at least 2-3 of those months being continuous." "Avoid throwing pitches other than fastballs and changeups." "Avoid playing for multiple teams at the same time." "Avoid playing catcher while not pitching." "Players should not pitch in multiple games on the same day." "Play other sports during the course of the year." "Monitor for other signs of fatigue." "No pitcher shall appear in a game as a pitcher for three consecutive days, regardless of pitch counts."
Ages 13 to 14 adds: 100 combined innings in 12 months; breaking pitches only "after developing consistent fastball and changeup."
Parents and athletes are responsible for applying the limits across every team and season the player is on.

### 6b. Acute versus chronic workload (Gabbett 2016, "The training-injury prevention paradox," British Journal of Sports Medicine)
The PMC copy (PMC4789704) was blocked by a captcha. Read via a secondary explainer: https://simplifaster.com/articles/train-more-without-injuries/

What the source says:
- Acute load is the most recent 7 days; chronic load is the rolling 28-day average the athlete is used to. The ratio of acute to chronic flags spikes.
- A ratio of about 0.8 to 1.3 is associated with the lowest injury risk; injury incidence rises above about 1.5.
- The ranges come mostly from adult field and court sports; the explainer cautions against assuming they transfer directly, notes the model ignores age, prior injury, sleep and school stress, and says to use it alongside other monitoring.

### 6c. AAP on overuse injuries, overtraining and burnout (Brenner, Pediatrics June 2007;119(6))
Read via American Family Physician summary: https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2007/1201/p1725.html

What the source says:
- "At least one day per week of rest from all organized physical activity." Limit a sport to five days a week counting games, practices and scrimmages.
- "At least two to three months off from sports per year."
- Weekly training time, repetitions and distance should not rise by more than 10 percent per week.
- "Children and their parents should be encouraged to participate on only one team per season"; travel-team time counts toward the limits.
- Overuse warning stages: pain after activity; pain during activity without limiting performance; pain during activity that limits performance; chronic pain even at rest. Overtraining signs: chronic muscle or joint pain, personality change, higher resting heart rate, fatigue, lack of enthusiasm.
- Two sports that stress the same body part (the example given is swimming plus pitching) raise overuse risk.

### 6d. AAP on sport specialization (Brenner, "Sports Specialization and Intensive Training in Young Athletes," Pediatrics 2016;138(3):e20162148)
Read via MDedge summary: https://mdedge.com/content/aap-report-warns-against-early-single-sport-specialization and Johns Hopkins Medicine: https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/expert-qa/youth-sport-specialization

What the sources say:
- Delay single-sport specialization until late adolescence, about 15 or 16.
- "At least 1-2 days off per week from the focal sport" lowers injury risk; "taking 1 month off at least 3 times a year" for recovery.
- "Don't play more hours per week than the athlete's age in years" (Jayanthi, Loyola, 2013: athletes who did were 70 percent more likely to have a serious overuse injury; source https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/04/130419132508.htm). Injury risk also rose when organized sport time was more than twice free-play time.
- NATA 2019 statement (https://www.nata.org/sites/default/files/youth_sports_specialization_recommendations.pdf): one organized sport per season, no more than eight months a year in one sport, hours per week not above age, "a minimum of two days off per week from organized training and competition," time away at the end of each season.

**What the app encodes from section 6:**
- Pitch log: the parent enters pitches thrown per game. The app applies the 11 to 12 table now and the 13 to 14 table from his 13th birthday, shows the required rest days, counts innings against the 12-month cap, and warns on three consecutive pitching days. It also tracks the four-month throwing break.
- Throwing day tiers (light, moderate, heavy, none) come from pitches thrown plus position throwing. A heavy day (51 or more pitches, or a game at shortstop or center after pitching) blocks loaded or high-rep shoulder, elbow and forearm work and swaps in scapular control and gentle mobility.
- Cumulative load: each day gets a simple load score (minutes times a perceived-effort number the parent or Eli picks). The engine compares the last 7 days to the last 28 and does not let the 7-day total run more than about 1.3 times the 28-day average, with 1.5 as a hard stop that converts the day to recovery. Because the ratio evidence is from older athletes, the app uses it as a brake only, never to push load up.
- Weekly hours: total organized sport plus app sessions should not exceed 12 hours a week (his age); the app warns at 12 and shows the number.
- At least one full rest day each week with no organized sport and no app session; the app places it after the heaviest day. During tournament weekends the rest day lands on the day after.
- Multi-day tournament: the day after is recovery only (walk, easy mobility, sleep nudge); strength resumes no sooner than 48 hours after the last game.
- Basketball days: after a logged intense basketball session (game or hard practice), no extra jumping, plyometric or hard leg work that day; the next day defaults to upper-body or mobility.
- Baseball and basketball overlap: when both sports are in season the app drops to one short strength session a week and leans on mobility and recovery.
- Overuse warning capture: the daily check-in asks about pain after, during or limiting play; any "pain during" answer stops progression and tells a parent; any "pain at rest" answer holds all sessions until a parent clears it.
- Season breaks: the app encourages 2 to 3 months a year with no organized sport and keeps the four-month throwing break visible.

## Rules the readiness engine and progression encode

Each rule names the source it rests on. "Design choice" marks where the app is stricter than, or extends beyond, what the source states.

1. Bodyweight and technique first; every movement is learned unloaded. (AAP handout 1d; NSCA 2009; 2014 consensus; KidsHealth)
2. 5 lb dumbbells unlock only after a parent or coach confirms technique on the bodyweight version across several sessions. 10 lb is a later option the parent selects by hand; it is never switched on automatically. (AAP handout: add weight only after technique is mastered, no more than 10 percent per week; NSCA 5 to 10 percent steps. The specific 5 lb and 10 lb values are a design choice.)
3. Youth rep range: 1 to 3 sets of 6 to 15 reps for strength; 1 to 3 sets of 3 to 6 for jumps and power. (NSCA 2009; AAP 8 to 15; 2014 consensus 6 to 12)
4. No 1-rep max, no maximal loads, no training to failure, no powerlifting, bodybuilding or ballistic barbell lifts. (AAP handout 1d; AAP 2001; NSCA 2009 on unsupervised max testing)
5. No loaded twisting or loaded spinal flexion; trunk work is bracing, carries and controlled rotation with bodyweight. (Design choice; rests on the AAP handout's warning about spinal disc and growth-plate injury in unsupervised lifting and the AAP 2001 note on apophyseal spine injuries.)
6. 48 hours minimum between comparable strength sessions; 2 to 3 strength days a week, never consecutive. (NSCA 2009: 48 to 72 hours; 2014 consensus: non-consecutive days)
7. One change at a time when progressing: reps, then sets, then load. (NSCA 2009; 2014 consensus "progressive and systematic variation")
8. Every session has a dynamic warm-up and a cool-down with easy stretching. (AAP 2020 summaries; AAP handout; NSCA 2009)
9. Stop on pain, dizziness, illness or feeling unwell, and tell a parent; nothing resumes after an injury or illness sign without a parent's go-ahead. (AAP handout "stop lifting at once if pain is felt"; AAP 2020 summary: evaluate any illness or overuse sign before resuming)
10. Heavy-throwing day: no loaded or high-rep shoulder, elbow or forearm work; mobility and scapular control only. (Pitch Smart rest-day structure and fatigue monitoring; AAP 2007 on sports stressing the same body part; the Armored Heat concept of matching the session to the throwing day)
11. Pitch counts and rest days follow the Pitch Smart table for his age; no pitching three days in a row; innings tracked against the yearly cap; four months off throwing each year. (MLB Pitch Smart)
12. After intense basketball: no extra jumping, plyometrics or hard leg work the same day. (CDC bone-strengthening already covered by the sport; AAP 2007 ten-percent and overuse rules; design choice on the same-day block)
13. Day after an all-day or multi-day tournament is recovery only; strength returns no sooner than 48 hours after the last game. (NSCA 48 to 72 hours; AAP 2007 rest and overtraining signs)
14. Cumulative load brake: 7-day load held near the 28-day average, warning above about 1.3 times, recovery day forced above 1.5. Used only to slow down, never to push up, because the evidence is from older athletes. (Gabbett 2016 via secondary source; caution noted)
15. Weekly organized hours plus app sessions not above his age in years (12 now, 13 next year). (Jayanthi 2013; AAP 2016 specialization report; NATA 2019)
16. One full rest day every week from all organized activity and app sessions. (AAP 2007; NATA 2019 asks for two)
17. One team per season; 2 to 3 months a year away from organized sport. (AAP 2007; Pitch Smart "avoid playing for multiple teams at the same time")
18. Sleep target 9 to 12 hours at age 12, 8 to 10 from age 13; short sleep lowers readiness and swaps strength for mobility. (AASM 2016, AAP endorsed)
19. Water first; sports drink only as an option after more than an hour of hard work or in heat; energy drinks never. (AAP 2011 sports and energy drinks report; KidsHealth)
20. No creatine and no routine protein powder in the default program; food first. (AAP HealthyChildren 2026 creatine answer; AAP 2016 PES report via summaries; NPR on AAP position)
21. Sessions are short (about 20 to 30 minutes) and count toward total training time. (AAP handout; AAP 2020 summaries on counting resistance training in total load)
22. Supervision: the app is a guide, not a coach; a parent is present or nearby for every loaded session, and the first run of any new movement. (AAP 2020 summaries; NSCA 2009: no qualified supervision, no resistance exercise)
23. Growth-spurt mode (design assumption, not a cited rule): a logged height jump shifts the next few weeks toward coordination and landing control and freezes load progressions.
24. Medical screening reminder shown once at setup and once a season: the AAP lists conditions that need a doctor's input before resistance training. The app does not collect or store those details. (AAP 2020 summaries; AAP handout)
